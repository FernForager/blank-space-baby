# The house voice

How a Triviela card should sound, for everyone who writes or rewords one: the deck writers, the editor and the desk. Written by Wordsmith #18 on Oct 31, 2026, after Morg asked for “our Mark Twain” and cards that “just feel really good”. Read it after `SPEC.md` (the format and the hard rules) and before `MORG-STYLE.md` (Morg’s own edits). Where they differ, CLAUDE.md’s house style wins, then Morg’s own wording, then this file. It grows with every taste test Morg answers.

## In one breath
A schoolbook sentence with a straight face. The card states one true thing plainly, and the fun comes from the answer itself or from one small, human detail beside it, never from the narrator winking. The meaning line underneath pays the player for answering: one new thing they didn’t know a second ago.

## Whose shoulders we stand on
We write in our own words and never quote anyone, but we know whose habits we borrow:
- **Mark Twain:** plain American speech and the comic power of understatement. The bigger the moment, the flatter the telling.
- **S. J. Perelman:** delight in the one exact, surprising word. Spent like a rare coin: one per card at most.
- **Eduardo Galeano:** the game as everyone’s story, the small people beside the big moment, memory as the point.
- **Brian Glanville:** the reporter’s precision and the long view, decades of the game held side by side.
- **Hugh McIlvanney:** weight and rhythm, a sentence built to land on its strongest word.
- **Gianni Brera:** a love of the game’s own vocabulary. We teach its words as carefully as he coined his.
- **Nelson Rodrigues:** the game as theater and the crowd as a character. We allow one dramatic detail, not two.
- **Jonathan Wilson:** tactics and positions explained so a new parent can follow them.
- **Simon Kuper:** the surprising number, and the curious reason behind the obvious fact.
- **Grant Wahl:** every league and the women’s game reported with the same seriousness as the biggest men’s club.

## The sentence
- **One fact, one sentence, the answer late.** A blank near the end lands like a punchline. A blank up front is fine for a name, as long as the sentence then earns it.
- **Show the answer, don’t define it.** A sentence that holds the answer’s definition plays itself, so the meaning line defines and the sentence shows the thing happening. Before: “To hit a volley, kick the ball out of the air before it bounces.” After: “The cross never touched the grass: the striker smashed it in on the volley.”
- **One human detail at most** (Dad’s coffee, Grandma’s old position, a phone that won’t stop ringing). It carries the fun and never the fact.
- **Concrete beats abstract:** a place, a year, a score, a name. Pick the detail a fan would retell.
- **No praise the source doesn’t give.** “Famous”, “legendary” and “greatest” appear only when the official source says so.
- **Read it out loud.** If it doesn’t sound like a person talking, cut words until it does. Under 70 characters when the fact allows.
- **The ear decides the tense and verbs:** plain past tense for what happened, present for what still holds, British plural verbs for clubs and national teams (“Lazio took”, “Arsenal are”).

## The meaning line
- **It is the reward.** One new thing: what the answer means, why it is so, or what happened next.
- **Never an echo.** A line that repeats the sentence, or spells out a club’s full name, teaches nothing. Before: “Portland Thorns FC”. After: “Home field: Providence Park”.
- **Aim for the “huh”.** Lazio’s colors come from the flag of Greece, and the line adds that the club began in 1900 as a running club. Real Madrid beat Leverkusen in Glasgow, and the line adds that a famous Zidane finish won it.
- **Kind-hearted history.** A teasing nickname is fair game when the club’s own fans have laughed along, as Leverkusen’s did with Neverkusen before their title ended it.

## The hook
The hook is a promise: it says what the card is about and never what the answer is. Take the most specific, most vivid word in the sentence (a player over a league, a city over a stadium’s sponsor), and vary it across a deck so two tiles never read alike. `SPEC.md` has the rules.

## Honest difficulty
- **Judge the card as it plays,** with its two wrong answers beside it. Green is easy because the fact is basic, never because the sentence gives it away.
- **Nothing silly-easy.** Three freebies to watch for: the sentence defines the answer (“as the tournament’s third-best player” for the Bronze Ball), the club’s name holds the answer (the Thorns of Portland), or the wrong answers can’t fit the blank. Move the clue into the meaning line, and send decoy problems to the desk.
- **Red is deep, never obscure.** A real fan should know it. Nobody should need a search engine.

## Wrong answers in range
A good wrong answer is a right answer to a nearby question: the same kind, the same era, the same scale. A keeper’s award draws keepers. A women’s club card draws women’s clubs. A 45-minute half draws minutes, not seconds. The kinds and decoys are the desk’s (and Decoys #17’s) to fix, so a writer notes what’s out of range rather than changing a kind. A sentence can still help: “wear her shoe” takes a singular decoy that “wear shoe” would not.

## Humor
- **The straight face.** The situation is funny, the narrator is not. No puns on the answer, no winks, and exclamation points left to the slang decks.
- **Family-sized:** the sideline, the minivan, halftime oranges, Grandma’s glory days, the long bus ride home. Kid-safe always.
- **Punch up, never down.** We tease the way a club’s own fans tease themselves, never a real person’s private life and never a child.

## History
- **The long view in a small space:** a first, a record, a before and after. One date per card is usually plenty.
- **Every league and both games** get the same care, from the Bundesliga’s Werkself to the NWSL’s Supporters’ Shield.

## Before you hand a card in
1. Read it out loud once.
2. Cover the blank: could someone who doesn’t know the fact still work it out? Then it’s a freebie.
3. Read the meaning line alone: does it teach something the sentence didn’t?
4. Read the two wrong answers in the blank: does each one fit the grammar and sound possible?
5. Check every fact against the deck’s official source (CLAUDE.md, “Sources per deck”).
